\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Black-box deep learning models are highly non-linear and can exhibit unpredictable malicious behaviors \cite{betley2025weird} or inconsistent performance \cite{luo2025beyond}.
Recent work addresses this by using LLMs to generate task-specific policy structures that are sparse and semantically meaningful \cite{zhu2025sample}, improving the efficiency and robustness of imitation learning.
However, these approaches rely heavily on human experts to provide domain knowledge or to continuously monitor and refine the generated structures.

In principle, LLMs already encode substantial task-relevant knowledge from pretraining and could be used to instantiate policy structures.
However, because LLMs do not directly observe the environment, the generated structures may encode modeling assumptions that conflict with expert demonstrations or overlook critical variable interactions.
For example, an LLM may introduce a damping term that links steering to angular velocity to correct spins.
However, in demonstrations, an expert may steer in the same direction as the angular velocity during sharp turns, causing the learned policy to violate the intended assumption and exhibit unwanted oscillatory behavior.

To resolve such misalignment, we propose a closed-loop framework that alternates demonstration-based parameter fitting with LLM-guided structural refinement (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).
The process resembles software debugging: a programmer specifies expected behavior, writes tests, and uses their results to locate and correct errors.
Here, the LLM generates data analysis code to test expected policy behavior, runs it on tabular rollouts, and uses the results to revise the structure before fitting its parameters again.
This debugging interface is possible because structured policies are sparse and semantically meaningful: each intermediate quantity has an interpretable role, and its dependencies can be traced to specific computations that the LLM can inspect and edit.
For the turning example above, tests can examine whether the angular-velocity term damps rotation as intended and whether steering oscillates during turns.
The results can guide updates to that term or its parameter constraints, such as using \texttt{softplus} to enforce an intended coefficient sign.
Unlike prior methods that rely on visual \cite{chen2024elemental} or textual feedback \cite{peng2024adaptive}, our approach executes analysis code outside the LLM on complete rollout tables and returns only the results, allowing it to handle trajectories beyond the LLM's context limit and data that are not easily visualizable.

Our contributions are threefold: (1) LLM-driven automated policy structure generation and refinement without human expert input, (2) the use of tabular rollout analysis as environment feedback for policy updates, and (3) empirical evidence that structure refinement improves the efficiency of reinforcement learning from demonstrations in control tasks.

\caption{Overview of policy refinement.
\textbf{1. Generate the initial structure} by querying an LLM with the task description.
\textbf{2. Update parameters} through imitation learning on the demonstrations.
\textbf{3. Generate analysis code} based on the desired behavior of the policy and its structure topology.
\textbf{4. Collect tabular trajectories}, recording observations, actions, and named latent values during policy rollouts.
\textbf{5. Execute analyses and revise the structure} based on the analysis results.
The updated program returns to parameter fitting and another refinement cycle.
\textbf{6. Reinforcement learning} after the structure aligns with the demonstrations to go beyond the demonstration performance.
